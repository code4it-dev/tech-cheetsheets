Little's Law

How many are inside = how fast they arrive × how long they stay. That's the whole thing.

As of Nov 2026 · proved in 1961 and not going to change; the examples are .NET 10 (LTS), PostgreSQL and Prometheus

Three quantities describe any system that things flow through: L, how many are inside at once; λ, how fast they arrive; and W, how long each one stays. Little's Law says they are not independent — L = λW — so knowing any two determines the third, always.

What makes it unusual is how few assumptions it needs. It does not care whether arrivals are smooth or bursty, whether service is fast or slow, whether the queue is FIFO or LIFO or random, or how many servers there are. It is not a model or an approximation: it is an identity, true of any system in steady state. John Little proved it in 1961, and the proof is essentially an accounting argument.

In practice it is the formula behind connection pool sizing, thread pool exhaustion, queue depth alarms, capacity estimates and most "why is this slow?" conversations. It is also one of the most misapplied results in our field — almost always because the person using it has not said which boundary they drew around the system, which is the subject of the fourth animation below.

The three forms, side by side

Same identity, rearranged. The middle column is what you are actually asking.

FormThe questionYou measureTypical useWhere it bites
L = λWhow many will be in flight?rate and latencysizing a connection or thread pool, setting a queue-depth alarmW must be the full time in your boundary, not service time
λ = L / Wwhat can this sustain?concurrency limit and latencycapacity from a fixed pool, deciding if a tier needs scalinggives a ceiling, not a safe operating point — see animation 3
W = L / λwhy is it slow?in-flight count and throughputfinding where time goes when your traces disagree with your dashboardsthe one people forget exists

Three things worth keeping:

  • The third form is the diagnostic one. If 200 requests are in flight and you are completing 1,000 a second, then the average request is spending 200 ms inside — no matter what your service's own timer reports. When that number disagrees with your latency dashboard, the gap is the queueing you are not measuring.
  • You cannot improve one number in isolation. Halving latency at constant traffic halves concurrency; holding concurrency while traffic doubles means latency halves or the system is not stable. The identity is a constraint, not a dial.
  • Little's Law sizes things; it does not tell you where to stop. It will happily tell you that 500 req/s at 2 s latency needs 1,000 concurrent slots. Whether you should ever operate there is the question animation 3 answers, and the answer is no.

Key concepts

L, lambda, WThe three quantities: items inside, arrival rate, time inside. Fix any two and the third is determined.
steady stateThe one precondition: over the window you measure, as many items leave as arrive. Without it there is no stable L to talk about.
the boundaryThe box you draw around "the system". Move it and all three numbers change — which is why an unstated boundary makes the law look broken.
service time vs residence timeW is the whole time inside the boundary — waiting plus being served. Substituting service time is the most common error.
utilisationρ = λ/μ, the fraction of capacity in use. Little's Law does not mention it, and that omission is the point.
the latency wallAs utilisation approaches 1, waiting time rises hyperbolically. Nothing got slower; there is simply nowhere for arrivals to go.
variabilityBursty arrivals or uneven service times move the wall closer. Kingman's formula puts a number on it.
pool sizingThe main thing people use the law for: how many connections, threads or permits you need to carry a given rate at a given latency.
thread starvationWhat happens when W grows and the pool is fixed: the pool fills, arrivals queue outside it, and the whole service stalls on one slow dependency.
nestingThe law applies at every layer at once, and λ is shared between them. That is what makes a latency budget add up.
measurement windowAll three quantities must come from the same interval, long enough for start-up and drain to wash out.
WIP limitsThe same identity outside software: cap work in progress and cycle time falls, because λ is fixed by demand.
unitsCarry the units through. If they do not cancel to "items", you have mixed two boundaries or two windows.
instabilityWhen arrivals exceed departures there is no steady state, L grows without bound, and the law has nothing to describe.
headroomThe gap between λ and μ. Little's Law tells you what you need; headroom is what keeps W off the wall.
measurementHow to actually get λ, W and L out of a running system, and how to use the law to check your instrumentation.

Essential formulas & numbers

The law, and the two things it does not say

L = λ × W                      # items inside = arrival rate × time inside
λ = L / W                      # the sustainable rate from a fixed pool
W = L / λ                      # where the time is actually going
One identity, three rearrangements. Carry the units: per-second with seconds, never with milliseconds.
ρ = λ / μ                      # utilisation: arrival rate over service rate
W = S / (1 - ρ)                # simple queue: residence time vs service time S
Little's Law never mentions ρ. This is what tells you where W comes from and why the last 10% of capacity is so expensive.
Wq ≈ (ρ / (1 - ρ)) × ((ca² + cs²) / 2) × S
Kingman: the middle term is the variability penalty. Bursty arrivals move the wall to a lower utilisation.

Sizing things

connections = peak_qps × seconds_per_query
            = 2000 × 0.040 = 80        → pool of 100
Use peak rate and the latency you see under load, not the one from an idle box.
threads = peak_rps × seconds_per_request
        = 500 × 0.200 = 100            → and ask why W is 200 ms
A large answer here is usually a signal to fix W rather than to raise the limit.
max_sustainable_rps = pool_size / seconds_per_request
                    = 100 / 0.020 = 5000     # and 100 / 0.5 = 200 when it degrades
The ceiling a fixed pool imposes. Watch what a 25× latency regression does to it.
workers = backlog / (throughput_per_worker × target_seconds)
The same identity for draining a queue by a deadline, rather than holding a steady state.

Measuring it with Prometheus

sum(rate(http_requests_total[5m]))
λ. Count everything that entered the boundary, including requests that ended in an error.
sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_sum[5m]))
  / sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count[5m]))
W, as the mean. Not histogram_quantile — Little's Law is about averages.
avg_over_time(http_requests_in_flight[5m])
L. Same window as the other two, or the arithmetic will not reconcile.
# the instrumentation check: this should sit very close to 1
sum(rate(http_requests_total[5m]))
  * (sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_sum[5m])) / sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count[5m])))
  / avg_over_time(http_requests_in_flight[5m])
If λW / L drifts from 1, one of the three metrics is wrong. The law makes a decent unit test for your telemetry.

In .NET

// the pool is a concurrency limit, and that is the point
"ConnectionStrings:Default": "Host=db;Maximum Pool Size=100;Timeout=3"
L is capped here. When W rises, λ falls — the identity has no other option.
static readonly UpDownCounter<int> InFlight =
    Meter.CreateUpDownCounter<int>("http.server.active_requests");

InFlight.Add(1);
try { await next(ctx); } finally { InFlight.Add(-1); }   // the finally is the whole point
Measuring L. A gauge that is not decremented on the error path is the most common reason the law "fails".
from prometheus_client import Gauge

IN_FLIGHT = Gauge("http_requests_in_flight", "L — requests inside the system")

@IN_FLIGHT.track_inprogress()        # increments on entry, decrements on exit
async def handle(request):
    return await do_work(request)

# then, from your own telemetry:
concurrency = qps * seconds_per_request        # L = λW
pool_size   = ceil(peak_qps * p99_seconds)     # size for the peak, not the mean
max_rps     = pool_size / seconds_per_request  # what that pool can actually sustain
L is the term people forget to measure. track_inprogress gives it to you directly, and the three sizing lines follow from it.
builder.Services.AddRateLimiter(o => o.AddConcurrencyLimiter("db", opt =>
{
    opt.PermitLimit = 80;          // L, chosen with the law
    opt.QueueLimit  = 20;          // a bounded queue, so W cannot run away
}));
Capping L deliberately instead of discovering it by exhausting a pool.

Numbers worth remembering

ρ = 0.5  → W ≈ 2 × S          ρ = 0.9  → W ≈ 10 × S
ρ = 0.8  → W ≈ 5 × S          ρ = 0.95 → W ≈ 20 × S
The shape of the wall. Flat for most of the range, then vertical.
1 request in flight  = 1 req/s × 1 s  = 1000 req/s × 1 ms
The sanity anchor: one unit of concurrency buys a thousand requests per second only if each takes a millisecond.

Pros & cons

What it gives you

  • An exact relationship, not an approximation — no distribution to assume and nothing to calibrate.
  • Pool and thread sizing in one multiplication, from numbers you already have.
  • A way to find queueing you are not instrumenting, by comparing end-to-end W with service time.
  • A unit test for your telemetry: if λW ≠ L, one of the three metrics is wrong.
  • It explains thread starvation and WIP limits with the same line of arithmetic.
  • It works at every layer at once, so latency budgets actually add up.

What it will not do

  • It says nothing about utilisation, so it cannot tell you where to stop.
  • It is about means only — your p99 is invisible to it.
  • It needs steady state, so it describes nothing during an overload.
  • It predicts no latency: W is an input, never an output.
  • Mixing boundaries or windows produces confident nonsense.
  • It cannot tell you why W is what it is; that needs queueing theory or a profiler.

Concrete use cases

Sizing a connection pool before launch

A service expects 2,000 queries/s at peak with 40 ms queries. The law gives 80 concurrent connections, so the pool is set to 100. Without it the team would have copied the framework default of 20 and discovered the ceiling — 500 queries/s — during the launch, as timeouts.

Diagnosing a latency mystery

Dashboards show a handler averaging 8 ms, yet users report slow pages. The in-flight gauge reads 200 and throughput is 1,000/s, so W = 200/1000 = 200 ms. The 192 ms gap is connection-pool wait that nothing was measuring, and the fix is the pool, not the query.

Explaining a capacity collapse

A downstream dependency degrades from 20 ms to 500 ms. The pool of 100 is fixed, so sustainable throughput falls from 5,000/s to 200/s — a 96% loss with no change to the service itself. The law turns an inexplicable outage into one line of arithmetic, and points at timeouts and bulkheads as the fix.

Draining a backlog by a deadline

Four million queued messages must clear in two hours. One worker handles 50/s, so one worker clears 360,000 in that window and twelve are needed. The law sizes the fleet; the utilisation curve then says to provision fifteen so the workers are not running at 100%.

Setting a WIP limit on a team board

A team finishes five stories a week with thirty in progress, so cycle time is six weeks. Capping WIP at ten changes nothing about how fast anyone works, but cycle time falls to two weeks — and the argument for the cap is an identity rather than an opinion.

When to use / when not to

If you know two of the three, you already know the third — whether or not you've measured it.

Reach for it when…

  • You need to size a connection pool, thread pool or concurrency limit.
  • Your in-flight count and your latency dashboard disagree.
  • Throughput collapsed and nothing in the service changed.
  • You want to know where in-flight work is actually sitting, layer by layer.
  • You need to sanity-check latency, throughput and concurrency metrics against each other.
  • You are arguing about work in progress, queue depth or batch size.

Don't lean on it when…

  • The system is overloaded. There is no steady state — use load shedding, then measure.
  • You care about the tail. It is an average; use percentiles.
  • You want to predict latency from load. W is an input — use queueing theory or a load test.
  • You need the effect of variability. Use Kingman's formula.
  • The workload is a mix of very different items. Split it and apply the law per class.
  • You cannot state the boundary. Fix that first, or the arithmetic is meaningless.

Common mistakes & gotchas

Using service time as WThe handler reports 8 ms, so 8 ms goes into the formula — but L was measured across the whole system, including everything waiting for a thread or a connection. The numbers do not reconcile and the law gets blamed.
Fix: W is the entire time inside the boundary you drew, waiting included. If you want to use service time, measure L at the servers only — and then L is just utilisation × server count.
Not stating the boundary at all"We have 100 in flight" — in the service, in the pool, in the database, or across all three? Each is a different L with a different W, and mixing two of them produces a confident wrong answer.
Fix: name the box every time, and remember λ is the only quantity shared between nested boundaries. Write the boundary down next to the number on the dashboard.
Mixing unitsλ in requests per second and W in milliseconds gives an L that is a thousand times too large, and it looks plausible enough to act on.
Fix: convert everything to seconds first and carry the units through the multiplication. If they do not cancel to plain items, stop.
Applying it during an overloadThe system is unstable, the queue is growing, and somebody divides the current L by the current λ to get a W that describes nothing. Both numbers are moving and there is no steady state.
Fix: establish stability first — shed load, add capacity or slow the arrivals. Then measure over a window where arrivals and departures balance.
Using a percentile for Wp99 latency goes into the formula because that is what the dashboard shows. The result overstates concurrency badly, because the mean and the p99 of a long-tailed distribution are nowhere near each other.
Fix: use the mean — rate(duration_sum) / rate(duration_count). If you want to size for the tail, that is a different and harder question than this identity answers.
Sizing a pool with idle-box latencyThe query takes 8 ms on a quiet machine, so the pool is sized for 8 ms. Under real load it takes 40 ms, the pool is five times too small, and the service hits its ceiling at a fifth of the expected throughput.
Fix: measure W at the load you are sizing for, from a load test or production. Latency and utilisation move together, which is exactly what animation 3 is about.
Treating a big L as the answerThe arithmetic says 400 concurrent connections, so the pool is set to 400. The application now pushes 400 concurrent queries at a database that was already the bottleneck, turning a queue in the app into a slower one in the database.
Fix: when L comes out large, treat it as a signal to reduce W. A pool is a concurrency limit protecting the thing behind it, and raising it rarely creates capacity that was not there.
Forgetting the finally on the in-flight gaugeL is incremented on entry and decremented on the happy path only. Every error leaks a count, the gauge drifts upward forever, and the law appears to break a few hours after deploy.
Fix: decrement in a finally, and alert when λW / L drifts away from 1 — it is a cheap, continuous check on your instrumentation.

  • Start with Little's Law for anything about pools, concurrency or in-flight work. It is one multiplication and needs no assumptions.
  • Add the utilisation curve the moment the question becomes "how hard can we push this", because the law will not warn you.
  • Reach for Kingman when traffic is bursty or request costs vary wildly — the simple curve will be optimistic.
  • Use Erlang C when the goal is a wait-time target across many servers, as in a worker fleet or a support rota.
  • Load test before committing. Every formula here assumes something; a test is the only thing that measures.
