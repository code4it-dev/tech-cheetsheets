Back-of-the-envelope estimates

Be roughly right in five minutes instead of precisely wrong in two weeks.

As of Nov 2026 · latency and cost anchors refreshed for NVMe, modern networks and current cloud list prices

Most architectural decisions do not need a benchmark. They need to know whether the answer is 10, 10,000 or 10 million — because those three answers lead to three completely different systems. A back-of-the-envelope estimate is a deliberately crude calculation, done in a few minutes with rounded numbers, whose purpose is to eliminate options rather than to predict a value.

The method is always the same: write down what you are estimating, break it into quantities you can guess within a factor of two, round everything to one significant figure, multiply, and then sanity-check the result against something you already know. Errors in opposite directions cancel, so an estimate built from five rough guesses is usually accurate to within a factor of two or three — far better than the precision of its inputs suggests.

It is the core skill in a system design interview, but it earns its keep in real work: deciding whether a table needs sharding, whether a feature's storage cost sinks its business case, whether a batch job can finish overnight, or whether the thing you were about to build is off by three orders of magnitude from what the hardware can do.

Key concepts

order of magnitudeThe power of ten an answer sits at. Getting that right is the whole job; the leading digit is a bonus.
rounding rulesRound every input to one significant figure, preferably to a power of ten, before multiplying. Precision you did not have is precision you cannot keep.
QPSQueries per second: daily volume divided by 86,400. The number almost every other number is derived from.
peak factorTraffic is never flat. Multiply the average by 2–4 for a normal daily peak, and far more for an event.
latency numbersThe ladder from L1 cache to an intercontinental round trip. Knowing the rungs stops you designing something physics forbids.
data sizesBytes per field, per row, per object. Estimate by adding up what is actually stored, then double it for overhead.
storage growthDaily volume times retention times replication. The number that most often kills a design, and the one most often left out of it.
bandwidthQPS multiplied by payload size. Convert to bits per second before comparing with any network specification.
Little's Lawconcurrency = arrival rate x latency. Three quantities; know two and the third is determined. It sizes pools and queues.
read:write ratioHow many reads per write. The single number that decides most of the architecture before any other consideration.
hot vs coldNot all data is accessed equally. Usually around 20% of it serves 80% of requests, and recent data dominates.
machines neededPeak load divided by what one machine does, plus headroom. The last step before the number becomes money.
headroomThe gap between what you provision and what you use. Running a system at 90% utilisation is a decision to have no response to anything.
cost anchorsRough unit prices, good to a factor of two. Enough to tell whether an idea is cheap, expensive or impossible.
sanity checkThe last step, and the one people skip: compare the answer with something you already know to be true.
sensitivityWhich assumption dominates the answer. Find it, and spend your remaining time on that one input only.

Essential formulas & numbers

The numbers to memorise

86,400 s/day      ~ 1 x 10^5      # the single most useful constant
2,600,000 s/month ~ 2.5 x 10^6
31,500,000 s/year ~ 3 x 10^7      # "pi x 10^7" is within 1%
Converting a daily or yearly volume into a per-second rate is the first step of nearly every estimate.
2^10 ~ 1 thousand    2^20 ~ 1 million    2^30 ~ 1 billion    2^40 ~ 1 trillion
1 KB = 10^3 B   1 MB = 10^6 B   1 GB = 10^9 B   1 TB = 10^12 B   1 PB = 10^15 B
Treat binary and decimal prefixes as equal. The 2.4% error per step is far below the precision of your inputs.
1 Gbps = 125 MB/s        # divide bits by 8
10 Gbps = 1.25 GB/s      # one busy server's worth
100 Gbps = 12.5 GB/s     # CDN territory
The bits-versus-bytes conversion, which is the most common factor-of-eight error in sizing work.

The formulas

qps       = daily_volume / 86,400
peak_qps  = qps x peak_factor           # 2-4 normally, 20-100 for an event
Two lines that produce the number everything else is derived from.
storage = items_per_day x bytes_per_item x retention_days x replicas x 1.5
The 1.5 is index and metadata overhead. Leaving it out is the usual reason an estimate is low.
bandwidth_bps = qps x payload_bytes x 8
Compute it for peak, not average, and separately for ingress and egress — they are rarely symmetric.
concurrency = qps x latency_seconds          # Little's Law
machines    = peak_qps / per_machine_qps / target_utilisation
Little's Law sizes pools and threads; the second line sizes the fleet. Use 0.5 for utilisation unless you know better.
cache_size = hot_fraction x daily_items x bytes_per_item    # hot_fraction ~ 0.1-0.2
Cache the hot slice, not the dataset. This is usually two orders of magnitude smaller than total storage.

Worked example, in Python

const long   Dau            = 100_000_000;
const double UploadsPerUser = 0.2;
const long   PhotoBytes     = 2_000_000;
const long   SecPerDay      = 100_000;        // 86,400, rounded for sanity

var writesPerDay = (long)(Dau * UploadsPerUser);   //  20M
var writeQps     = writesPerDay / SecPerDay;       // 200 /s
var perDay       = writesPerDay * PhotoBytes;      //  40 TB
var fiveYear     = perDay * 365 * 5 * 3;           //  x3 replicas -> ~220 PB
var monthlyUsd   = fiveYear / 1e9 * 0.02;          //  $/GB/month

Console.WriteLine($"{fiveYear / 1e15:F0} PB, ${monthlyUsd / 1e6:F1}M per month");

foreach (var uploads in new[] { 0.05, 0.2, 1.0 })  // sensitivity on the weakest input
    Console.WriteLine($"{uploads,5} -> {Dau * uploads * PhotoBytes * 365 * 5 * 3 / 1e15,7:F0} PB");
The same estimate in C#. Named consts keep the units visible, which is what stops an order-of-magnitude slip.
DAU          = 100_000_000
uploads_user = 0.2
views_user   = 50
photo_bytes  = 2_000_000
SEC_PER_DAY  = 100_000          # 86,400, rounded for sanity

writes_day = DAU * uploads_user          # 20,000,000
reads_day  = DAU * views_user            # 5,000,000,000

write_qps = writes_day / SEC_PER_DAY     # 200
read_qps  = reads_day  / SEC_PER_DAY     # 50,000
print(f"peak: {write_qps*3:.0f} w/s, {read_qps*3:.0f} r/s, ratio {read_qps/write_qps:.0f}:1")
Average and peak QPS plus the read:write ratio — enough to decide the shape of the system.
per_day   = writes_day * photo_bytes           # 40 TB
five_year = per_day * 365 * 5 * 3              # x3 replicas -> ~220 PB
monthly   = five_year / 1e9 * 0.02             # $/GB/month -> ~$4.4M
print(f"{five_year/1e15:.0f} PB, ${monthly/1e6:.1f}M per month")
The number that decides whether the product is viable, reached in four lines.
for uploads in (0.05, 0.2, 1.0):               # sensitivity on the weakest input
    pb = DAU * uploads * photo_bytes * 365 * 5 * 3 / 1e15
    print(f"{uploads:>5} uploads/user/day -> {pb:7.0f} PB")
Varying the dominant assumption. If every case lands in the same regime, the estimate is done.

Quick reference anchors

1 PB hot object storage   ~ $20,000 / month
1 PB internet egress      ~ $50,000
1 TB on one SSD           ~ fits; "big data" tooling is a liability below this
Three facts that settle a surprising number of architecture debates on their own.
fibre: ~200 km per ms  ->  London-New York (5,600 km) = 28 ms one way, 56 ms RTT minimum
The speed-of-light floor. No budget removes it; only a replica nearer the user does.

Pros & cons

Pros

  • Costs five minutes and can eliminate weeks of building the wrong thing.
  • Makes assumptions explicit and arguable, instead of buried in someone's intuition.
  • Catches order-of-magnitude errors, which are the ones that actually sink designs.
  • Needs no data, no tooling and no access — it works in a meeting, on a whiteboard.
  • Produces the vocabulary for the next conversation: QPS, PB, peak factor, cost per month.
  • Identifies which single measurement is worth taking, which is often the real output.

Cons

  • Wrong inputs produce confident wrong answers, and the confidence survives longer than the inputs.
  • Says nothing about tail behaviour, contention, or how a system fails — only about averages.
  • Easy to anchor on: once a number is on the whiteboard, people stop questioning it.
  • Ignores everything it did not model: operational load, migrations, compliance, people.
  • Tempting to over-refine, which adds precision but no accuracy.
  • Rounds away real effects — a 20% overhead disappears, but you still pay it.

Concrete use cases

Deciding whether to shard

A team is planning a sharding project. The estimate: 50M rows a year at 500 B is 25 GB a year, 125 GB over five years, with indexes maybe 250 GB. That comfortably fits one Postgres instance with room to spare. The sharding project is cancelled in ten minutes, and the effort goes into the index that was actually slow.

Killing a feature before it is built

Product wants to keep full request and response bodies for every API call, for a year. 200M calls a day at 4 KB is 800 GB a day, 290 TB a year, roughly $6,000 a month before replication. The conversation changes from "can we" to "we can keep 30 days of sampled bodies for $500" — which turns out to meet the actual requirement.

Sizing a connection pool

A service handles 2,000 req/s with a 40 ms database round trip. Little's Law gives 80 concurrent queries, so a pool of 100 is right and the configured 500 is actively harmful — it lets the service overwhelm the database instead of queueing politely in front of it.

Checking that a batch job fits in its window

A nightly reconciliation processes 80M records. At 5,000 records/s per worker it needs 16,000 worker-seconds — four and a half hours on one worker, 30 minutes on ten. The six-hour window holds, but with only one worker there is no margin, and the estimate justifies the parallelism before the first missed morning.

Answering "can we serve this from one region?"

Users in Sydney, servers in Dublin: 16,000 km, so at least 160 ms round trip and realistically 250 ms. A page needing four sequential round trips is a one-second page before any work happens. The estimate makes the case for a read replica or an edge cache without needing to deploy anything to prove it.

When to use / when not to

If the estimate and the real thing disagree by 20%, the estimate did its job. By 100x, it saved the project.

Estimate when…

  • You are choosing between architectures and want to eliminate the impossible ones.
  • Someone says "that won't scale" or "that's too expensive" without a number attached.
  • You need to decide whether to measure at all, and what to measure first.
  • A design depends on a quantity nobody has written down.
  • You are in a system design interview, where the estimate is the answer.
  • A cost or capacity number must go in a document that other people will act on.

Don't rely on it when…

  • You can simply measure. Real traffic beats any estimate — use a load test or production metrics.
  • The question is about tails, contention or failure modes. Use percentiles and real traces.
  • The decision is irreversible and expensive. Estimate to shortlist, then prototype.
  • The answer sits within 2x of a threshold. That is inside the error bars — benchmark it.
  • You are negotiating a contract or an SLA. Commitments need measurements, not napkins.
  • The dominant cost is human: migration, on-call, training. Arithmetic does not model those.

Common mistakes & gotchas

Sizing for the average instead of the peak50,000 reads/s average sounds like a capacity plan. The evening peak is 150,000, so the system is at triple its provisioned load every night and nobody knows why the 20:00 graphs look like that.
Fix: multiply by 2–4 for a normal daily peak, and much more for events. Size for peak, budget for average, and state both.
Bits and bytes"10 Gbps is 10 GB/s" is an eight-fold error, and because bandwidth is often multiplied by time or price, the mistake compounds into a plan that is off by an order of magnitude.
Fix: convert once, early, and carry the units on every line. 1 Gbps = 125 MB/s. Write it at the top of the page.
Forgetting replication, indexes and backups"40 TB a day for five years is 73 PB." With three replicas, index overhead and backups it is closer to 250 PB — and the cost conclusion flips from uncomfortable to impossible.
Fix: multiply raw storage by about 3 as a default, and say which multipliers you applied. Replication and backups are not optional extras.
Refining inputs you already know wellTen minutes spent deciding whether a row is 180 or 220 bytes, in an estimate whose uploads-per-user guess could be off by 5x. The precision is real and completely wasted.
Fix: find the input with the widest uncertainty and work on that one. Run the estimate with a high and a low value for it; if the regime does not change, you are finished.
Treating the estimate as a measurementThe number lands in a design document, loses its assumptions on the way, and six months later is being quoted in a contract as though somebody measured it.
Fix: write the assumptions next to the number, always, and give it a range rather than a point. "40–400 PB, driven by uploads per user" ages far better than "220 PB".
Assuming linear scaling"One box does 1,000 QPS, so a hundred boxes do 100,000." Coordination, shared databases, locks and chatty services mean real scaling flattens, and sometimes reverses.
Fix: add 20–50% headroom for coordination beyond a handful of machines, and find the shared resource — usually the database or a lock — before trusting the multiplication.
Estimating storage but not egressThe storage bill is calculated and approved. Nobody computes serving cost, and the egress line arrives at several times the storage line because the data is not just stored, it is read constantly.
Fix: always estimate storage and transfer. Egress at $0.05–0.09 per GB is often the largest number on the page, and a CDN is the usual answer.
Skipping the sanity checkAn arithmetic slip gives a number implying every human on Earth uses the product hourly. The digits look plausible, so it goes into the deck unchallenged.
Fix: finish every estimate by comparing with something known — world population, a competitor's published scale, the product's revenue, the fastest disk that exists.

  • Measure instead whenever the system exists. An estimate of something you could query is wasted effort.
  • Load test once the estimate says you are within 2x of a limit — that is inside the error bars.
  • Reach for queueing theory when the question is "what happens at 80% utilisation", because the answer is not linear and the envelope cannot see it.
  • Prototype when the uncertainty is about whether something works at all, not about how much of it you need.
  • The healthy sequence is: estimate to shortlist, measure or prototype to confirm, load test before committing.
