EventStorming

Put everyone who knows something in front of one very long wall, and find out what the business actually does.

As of Nov 2026 · invented by Alberto Brandolini, 2013 · a workshop format, not a notation

EventStorming is a workshop. Domain experts and developers stand at a wall of paper, write things that happen in the business on orange sticky notes — in the past tense, one per note — and arrange them along a timeline. There is no upfront diagram, no notation to learn before you start, and no single person driving. In a morning a group that could not previously explain the domain has a shared picture of it on the wall.

What makes it effective is not the stickies but the format. Starting from events rather than entities keeps the conversation on what the business does instead of what the database stores. An enforced timeline makes gaps and contradictions physically visible. And writing disagreement onto a red hotspot rather than resolving it on the spot means the workshop surfaces the things nobody knew, which is usually its most valuable output.

It comes in three levels: Big Picture to explore a whole business, Process Modelling to design a single flow, and Design Level to work out the software for one slice — and at that last level the stickies map one-to-one onto code: commands, aggregates, domain events, policies and read models. That is why it is usually taught alongside Domain-Driven Design, though it is a useful technique on its own.

Architecture / mental model

Seven colours, one timeline, and a rule that every note is a single fact. Everything else about the method is facilitation.

Key concepts

Domain eventSomething that happened in the business, written in the past tense on an orange sticky — the atom of the whole method.
Chaotic explorationThe opening phase: everyone writes events at once, in silence, without agreeing an order or a format first.
TimelineArranging the events left to right in the order they happen — the move that turns a wall of notes into a model.
HotspotA red sticky placed on a disagreement, an unknown or a risk — parked rather than resolved.
Pivotal eventA moment where the business visibly changes phase — marked with a vertical line that often becomes a boundary.
CommandA blue sticky naming an intention in the imperative — the thing a person or system tried to do.
ActorA small yellow sticky naming the person or role that issues a command, and why they would.
AggregateA big yellow sticky between a command and its event: the chunk of business that decides whether the command is allowed.
PolicyA lilac sticky stating a reaction: "whenever X happened, then do Y" — the glue between one event and the next command.
Read modelA green sticky naming the information an actor looks at before issuing a command.
External systemA pink sticky for anything outside the boundary that emits or receives events — and that you cannot change.
Big PictureThe first level: a whole business or product explored in one session, with the widest possible group of people.
Process ModellingThe middle level: one end-to-end flow worked out in detail, adding commands, policies, actors and read models.
Design-LevelThe deepest level: one aggregate at a time, producing a model you can type in almost directly.
Bounded context discoveryReading candidate boundaries off the finished wall, where the language and the people change.
Unlimited modelling spaceThe deliberate constraint that the wall must never run out — because scarcity makes people stop writing.
FacilitationThe small set of behaviours that decide whether the session works: timeboxing, parking arguments, and keeping the loudest person off the wall.

Running one — the practical bits

Materials

8 m  butcher paper, taped at standing height (never a whiteboard)
500+ orange stickies   domain events      "Order Placed"
200  blue              commands           "Place Order"
100  lilac / purple    policies           "whenever... then..."
100  yellow            aggregates (big) + actors (small)
100  green             read models
 50  pink              external systems
 50  red               hotspots  - rotate 45 degrees
     one marker per person, same colour, thick tip
     NO chairs
The quantities are not a joke: running out of orange ends the exploration phase early. One marker each means everybody writes.

A Big Picture run sheet

00:00  framing          what we are exploring, and the one rule: past tense
00:10  chaotic explore  everyone writes events, in silence, in parallel
00:35  enforce timeline group arranges left to right; duplicates surface
01:15  walk the wall    narrate end to end; gaps and hotspots appear
01:45  break
02:00  pivotal events   mark phase changes with vertical lines
02:20  boundaries       where language and people change, draw a line
02:50  hotspot review   read every red note aloud; assign an owner
03:20  close            photograph everything, agree the next session
Timeboxed, and the walk-the-wall step is the one never to skip — it is where the gaps become obvious.
# facilitator phrases that do most of the work
"Past tense, please."
"That's a hotspot — write it red and we'll come back."
"What happened just before that?"
"Who did that? What were they looking at when they decided?"
"Everyone write, nobody talk, for five minutes."
Five sentences cover roughly 90% of facilitation. The rest is keeping time and keeping people standing.

Remote sessions

canvas      effectively infinite; never "zoom to fit"
groups      break out into 3-4 people; plenary only to walk the wall
timing      shorter blocks, more of them - 90 min max per block
tooling     Miro / Mural / FigJam; lock the legend, not the notes
camera      everyone on, or the parallel-writing phase dies quietly
Remote loses the standing-and-pointing dynamic, so compensate with smaller groups and shorter, more frequent blocks.

From wall to code

// blue  "Place Order"          -> command + handler
public sealed record PlaceOrder(Guid CartId, Guid CustomerId);

// yellow "Order"               -> aggregate root, holding the invariant
public sealed class Order { public void Place(...) { /* rule here */ } }

// orange "Order Placed"        -> domain event
public sealed record OrderPlaced(Guid OrderId, DateTime At);

// lilac  "whenever placed,     -> process manager / saga
//         reserve the stock"
public sealed class ReservationProcess : IHandle<OrderPlaced> { }

// green  "basket + stock"      -> projection on the read side
// pink   "payment provider"    -> anti-corruption layer
A design-level wall transfers to code almost mechanically — which is the strongest argument for running one before a big build.
# blue  "Place Order"      -> command + handler
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class PlaceOrder:
    cart_id: UUID
    customer_id: UUID

# orange "Order Placed"    -> domain event
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class OrderPlaced:
    order_id: UUID
    at: datetime

# yellow "Order"           -> aggregate root, holding the invariant
class Order:
    def place(self, cmd: PlaceOrder) -> list[OrderPlaced]: ...

# lilac  "whenever placed, reserve the stock" -> process manager
class ReservationProcess:
    def handle(self, event: OrderPlaced) -> None: ...

# green  "basket + stock"  -> projection      # pink "payment provider" -> ACL
The wall transfers just as mechanically in Python: frozen dataclasses for commands and events, a class per aggregate, a handler per policy.
# what to keep afterwards
photos/          high-res, overlapping, left to right (the model IS the photo)
hotspots.md      every red note, with an owner and a date
glossary.md      the words that won the vocabulary fights
contexts.md      candidate boundaries + the signal that suggested each
The wall decays within days. These four artefacts are what survives the session and what the next one starts from.

Pros & cons

Pros

  • Extremely fast: a morning produces understanding that weeks of interviews and documents usually do not.
  • No notation to learn, so domain experts contribute directly instead of reviewing someone's diagram.
  • Parallel writing gets contributions from quiet people and avoids the loudest voice setting the model.
  • Surfaces what nobody knew — hotspots are often more valuable than the timeline itself.
  • Finds bounded contexts before you commit them to services, which is when they are cheap to move.
  • At design level the output maps almost mechanically onto code.

Cons

  • Needs the right people in one room at one time, which is often the hardest part to arrange.
  • Facilitation-dependent: a weak facilitator produces an expensive, inconclusive meeting.
  • The artefact decays — a wall of stickies is not documentation and will not survive the week.
  • Works poorly remotely without deliberate adaptation.
  • Can drift into solution design the moment someone says "so we'd have a microservice that…".
  • Little value where the domain is genuinely simple or already well understood.

Concrete use cases

Before splitting a monolith

A team is about to carve services out of a monolith and cannot agree where. One Big Picture session shows the language changing at Order Placed and again at Parcel Delivered, and that the customers table is used by three groups who mean different things by "customer". The split follows the wall, not the schema.

Onboarding into an opaque domain

A new team inherits an insurance product nobody can explain end to end. Three hours with underwriting, claims and finance produces a timeline, nineteen hotspots and a glossary — and the hotspots become the first sprint's questions rather than its bugs.

Specifying a complex flow

"How do refunds work?" has four different answers in the company. A process-modelling session walks every branch — partial, post-shipment, expired card, goodwill — and the lilac policies turn directly into the process manager that gets built.

Finding the integration risk early

Halfway through the wall, the pink stickies cluster: six external systems in one phase, three of them batch-only overnight. The schedule risk is visible on day one instead of in month four.

Settling a vocabulary war

Two teams have argued for months about "booking" versus "reservation". On the wall it becomes obvious they are different things at different points in the timeline. Both words survive, in different contexts, and the argument ends.

When to use / when not to

if you can get the right eight people for three hours, it is almost always worth it

Use it when…

  • Nobody can explain the whole process end to end, and the documents disagree.
  • You are about to draw boundaries — services, modules, team ownership.
  • Domain experts and developers are talking past each other.
  • A new team needs to understand a complex domain quickly.
  • A flow is about to be rebuilt and its real branches are undocumented.

Avoid it when…

  • The domain is simple CRUD and everyone already agrees. Use a whiteboard and twenty minutes.
  • You cannot get domain experts in the room. Use interviews and Domain Storytelling until you can.
  • The question is about UI or user journeys. Use user story mapping.
  • The question is technical, not domain — throughput, storage, deployment. Use an architecture review or an ADR.
  • Someone senior has already decided the model and wants it ratified. Use honesty — the workshop will not survive a predetermined answer.

Common mistakes & gotchas

Not past tenseOnce Order Management and Update Customer appear on orange notes, the wall stops being a timeline and becomes a feature list — and the sequencing step no longer works.
Fix: correct it out loud every time in the first ten minutes. After that the room self-polices, and the rule costs nothing.
Resolving every argumentOne contested detail can eat ninety minutes while fifteen people watch two of them debate, and the wall stops growing.
Fix: red hotspot, rotated 45 degrees, owner's name, move on. Collecting the unknowns is a legitimate — often the best — outcome.
Missing the person who actually knowsA beautiful wall built without the one operations specialist is confidently wrong, and the error is discovered after the design is committed.
Fix: before booking, list who must be there and treat absence as a reason to reschedule. A gap in the timeline usually means a missing person, not a missing event.
Designing the solutionWithin an hour someone says "so we'd have a service that…" and the room switches from describing the business to designing software, which is a different and much less informed conversation.
Fix: ask "what happened?", never "what should we build?". Park solution ideas on a separate note and off the timeline.
A board that is too smallLimited space silently tells people to be selective, and selectivity is exactly what kills the exploration phase.
Fix: more paper than you can possibly use, at standing height, and remove the chairs. Remotely: a huge canvas and never "zoom to fit".
Tidying as you goDeduplicating early destroys the evidence: two notes with different words for the same thing is the vocabulary problem you came to find.
Fix: let duplicates accumulate during exploration. Merge them later, out loud, and record which word won.
No follow-upThe photos sit in a chat thread, the hotspots are never answered, and in three weeks the session is remembered as a nice day out.
Fix: leave with hotspots written up, each with an owner and a date, plus a glossary and a candidate context list. Book the next session before people leave the room.
Expecting documentationA wall of stickies is a thinking tool, not an artefact. It falls off the wall, and the photo is unreadable in two months.
Fix: convert the durable parts the same day — glossary, hotspot list, candidate boundaries, and for design-level sessions the code skeleton itself.

Alternatives

TechniqueBest forComplexityCost / licenseKey difference vs EventStorming
EventStormingexploring a whole domain fast, finding boundariesLow to run, high to facilitatefree; paper and people—
Domain Storytellingunderstanding one concrete scenario end to endLowfreePictographic story told by one expert; calmer, narrower, easier with few people
Event Modelingturning a flow into a buildable specificationMediumfreeMore prescriptive, includes UI wireframes and a strict swimlane layout
User story mappingplanning releases around user journeysLowfreeOrganised by user goals and slices, not business events; a planning tool
Example Mappingpinning down the rules of one storyLowfreeTiny scope, 25 minutes, produces concrete examples and test cases
Wardley Mappingdeciding what to build, buy or outsourceMediumfreeStrategy and evolution, not process; answers a different question entirely
Interviews and documentswhen you cannot get people togetherLowtimeSerial, slow, and never surfaces disagreement between experts
  • Pick Domain Storytelling when you have one or two experts and one concrete scenario, rather than a crowd and a whole domain.
  • Pick Event Modeling when the goal is a specification to build from, including screens.
  • Pick user story mapping for release planning — it answers "what do we ship first?", not "what does the business do?".
  • Pick Example Mapping for the rules of a single story, right before implementation.
  • Pick Wardley Mapping for build-versus-buy and strategic positioning; it pairs well, answering a question EventStorming does not ask.
